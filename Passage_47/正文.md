# Passage 47

In today's mad rush for success, many people believe that working to the maximum is the secret to success. Sometimes, taking breaks is seen as being stupid. Employees often find themselves working late into midnight hours, living in a cruel cycle without liberty to manage their personal time. However, life isn't about burning yourself out in pursuit of wealth or status. Research has found that moderation also gets results on the job.

In a study led by Ellen Langer of Harvard University, researchers asked people to translate sentences into a new made-up language. Subjects who practiced the language moderately beforehand made fewer errors than those who practiced extensively or not at all. High levels of knowledge can make people too attached to traditional ways of viewing problems in domains such as the arts, sciences, and politics. High conscientiousness creates an emergency state of constant productivity, which is related to lower job performance, especially in jobs where it doesn't pay to be a perfectionist, whether you're a secretary or a police officer.

How long we stay on the clock and how we spend that time sitting on the chair are under careful examination in many workplaces. The young bank clerk who eats lunch at his desk is probably seen as a go-getter, while his colleagues who chat over a relaxed conference-room meal get dirty looks from the corner office. "People from cultures that value relationships more than ours does are shocked by the thought of eating alone in front of a computer," says Art Markman, a professor at the University of Texas, Austin. The workplace is more than a place to earn a wage. Social interaction extends far beyond committee meetings. It has been shown to lift mood and get people thinking in new directions. The seemingly innocent exchanges such as sharing a slice of pancake or gathering for lovely conversations on Friday afternoon create invaluable connections that contribute to organizational success.

Markman also promotes off-task time. "Part of being a good thinker is experiencing things that are seemingly unrelated to what you are working on at the moment but give you fresh ideas about your work," he says. "Also, there is a lot of research showing that a positive mood leads to higher levels of productivity and creativity. So, when people do things to increase their life satisfaction, they also make themselves more effective at work."
