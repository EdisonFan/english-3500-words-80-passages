# Passage 74

"I am not crazy," says Dr. William Farber, shortly after performing acupuncture (针灸) on a rabbit. "I am ahead of my time." If he seems a little defensive, it might be because even some of his coworkers occasionally laugh at his unusual methods. But Farber is certain he'll have the last laugh. He's one of a small but growing number of American veterinarians (兽医) now practicing "holistic" medicine—combining traditional Western treatments with acupuncture, chiropractic (按摩疗法) and herbal medicine.

Farber, a graduate of Colorado State University, started out as a more conventional veterinarian. He became interested in alternative treatments 20 years ago when he suffered from terrible back pain. He tried muscle-relaxing drugs but found little relief from the pain. Then he tried acupuncture, an ancient Chinese practice, and was amazed that he improved after two or three treatments. This comprehensive approach has shown remarkable results in treating various conditions, from domestic pets to farm animals like cattle and pigs. So, after spending a couple of years studying the techniques, he began offering them to sick pets.

In a representative clinic setting, veterinary surgeons begin with a thorough inspection. Then they might use needles for injections (注射) and give pills. Whether treating a neat house cat or a humble farm cow, it's hard to keep the animals calm. However, they tend to be more comfortable with gentle methods like chiropractic. Veterinarians have reported success in treating conditions affecting the stomach and other organs.

Leigh Tindale's dog Charlie had a serious heart condition. After Charlie had a heart attack, Tindale was prepared to put him to sleep, but Farber's treatments eased her dog's suffering so much that she was able to keep him alive for an additional five months. Sarah Wilson's elderly cat Mittens suffered from severe joint problems that made climbing the stairs impossible. After six weeks of acupuncture, Mittens could freely move between floors. "She's like a teenager again." Wilson said. A deer named Buck developed an odd infection that regular medicine couldn't cure. The owner turned to the same treatments. Within three weeks, Buck returned to his normal lively self.

Farber is certain that the holistic approach will grow more popular with time and if the past is any indication, he may be right: Since 1982, membership in the American Holistic Veterinary Medical Association has grown from 30 to over 700. "Sometimes it surprises me that it works so well," he says. "I will do anything to help an animal. That's my job.
