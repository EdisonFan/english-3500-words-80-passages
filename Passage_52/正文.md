# Passage 52

In recent years, researchers from diverse fields have agreed that short-termism is now a significant problem in industrialized societies. The immediate gratification fever has infected everything from capital markets to private life, causing some of the world's most serious problems: climate change, biodiversity collapse, and more. The historian Francis Cole argues that the West has entered a period where "only the present exists, a present characterized at once by the cruelty of the instant and by the boredom of an unending now".

It has been proved that people have a bias (偏向) towards the present, focusing on loud attractions in the moment at the expense of health, well-being and financial stability of their future selves or community. We use disposable tissues and plastic chopsticks for the immediate convenience despite the problems they cause. In business, this bias surfaces as short-sighted decisions. And on slow-burning problems like climate change, it translates into the unwillingness to make small sacrifices (牺牲) today that could make a major difference tomorrow. Instead, the premier concern is next quarter's profit, or a deadline in September. As a result, the construction of new housing projects and ports may receive more attention than the conservation of forests and wetlands.

These biased perspectives cannot be blamed on one single cause. It is fair to say, though, that our psychological biases play a major role. People's hesitancy to delay satisfaction is the most obvious example. Think of how a quick discount has urged you to purchase. But there are other biases to be mentioned. One of them is about how the most accessible information in the present affects decisions about the future. For instance, you might hear someone say: "It's cold this winter, so I needn't worry about global warming." Another is that loud and urgent matters are given too much importance, making people ignore longer-term trends that arguably matter more. This is when a pop star's new hit draws far more attention than, say, the twin crises of climate change and gradual biodiversity decline.

As a psychologist once joked, if aliens (外星人) wanted to weaken humanity, they wouldn't send ships; they would invent climate change. Indeed, when it comes to environmental transformations, we can develop a form of collective "poor memory", and each new generation can believe the state of affairs they encounter is nothing out of the ordinary. Older people today, for example, can remember a time with insect-covered car windscreens after long drives. Children, on the other hand, have no idea that insect population has dropped dramatically.
