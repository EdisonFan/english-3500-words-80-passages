# Passage 78

What is life? Like most great questions, this one is easy to ask but difficult to answer. We know of just one type of life and it's challenging to do science with a sample size of one. The field of artificial life—called ALife for short—seeks to clone and simulate life-like (栩栩如生的) processes in an attempt to spell out life's fundamental principles. Many of these practitioners, so-called ALifers, think that somehow creating life is the surest way to really understand what life is.

So far, no one has convincingly made artificial life. This track record makes ALife a ripe target for criticism, such as declarations of the field's doubtful scientific value. Alan Smith, a complexity scientist, is tired of such complaining critics. "The existence of a living system is not about the use of anything." Alan says. "Some people ask me, 'So what's the worth of artificial life?' Do you ever think, 'What is the worth of your grandmother?'"

As much as many ALifers hate emphasizing their research's applications, the attempts to create artificial life could have practical pay-offs. Artificial intelligence may be considered ALife's cousin in that researchers in both fields are enamored (使倾心) by a prospect called open-ended evolution. This is the capacity for a system to create essentially endless complexity, to be a sort of "novelty generator". The only system known to exhibit this is Earth's biosphere. If the field of ALife manages to reproduce life's endless "creativity" in some virtual model, those same principles could give rise to truly inventive machines.

Compared with the developments in AI, advances in ALife are harder to recognize. One reason is that ALife is a field in which the central concept—life itself—is undefined. The lack of agreement among ALifers doesn't help either. Some think it's enough for a system to be called "life" if it can import energy, export waste, and evolve over time. Others may look backward at traditional definitions and stick to stricter standards. The result is a diverse line of projects that each advances along its unique path. For better or worse, ALife mirrors the very subject it studies. Its muddled (混乱的) progression is a striking parallel to the evolutionary struggles that have shaped Earth biosphere.

As the field continues to evolve, each new episode of research brings fresh insights. Undefined and uncontrolled, ALife has forced us to separate concepts considered to be characteristics of life and examine them more carefully. It may be, of course, that these characteristics aren't in any way surprising or singular. They may apply universally to all acts of evolution. Ultimately ALife may be nothing special. But even this dismissal suggests something: perhaps, just like life itself throughout the universe, the rise of ALife will prove unavoidable.
