# Passage 70

In 1916, two girls of wealthy families, best friends from Auburn, N. Y.—Dorothy Woodruff and Rosamond Underwood—traveled to a settlement in the Rocky Mountains to teach in a remote one-room schoolhouse with only a blackboard and some basic supplies. While most of their peers ( 同龄人) chose to marry or engage in dull social activities, the two women embarked on a journey that would take them far from the parties, fine silk clothing and the buffets they were used to, leaving behind the estate of their rich families. So for them to move to Elkhead, Colorado, to instruct the children, whose shoes were held together with string, was a surprise. The mountains of Colorado, dotted with mines and wandering goats, were a stark ( 鲜明的) contrast to their daily life. Their stay in Elkhead is the subject of Nothing Daunted: The Unexpected Education of Two Society Girls in the West, by Dorothy Wickenden-a magazine editor and Dorothy Woodruff's granddaughter.

Why did they go then? Well, they wanted to do something useful. Soon, however, they realized what they had undertaken.

They moved in with a local family, the Harrisons, and, like them, had little privacy, rare baths, and a blanket of snow on their quilt when they woke up in the morning—harsh conditions that would make most society men or women quit. Some mornings, Rosamond and Dorothy would arrive at the schoolhouse to find the children weeping from the cold. In spring, the snow was replaced by mud over ice. One can imagine them learning to sew their own clothes, and perhaps mending their students’ torn shirts and shorts. They wrote letters home, sharing their experiences with their families and brothers back east. They never showed pity, instead embracing their new life with determination.

In Wickenden's book, she expanded on the history of the West and also on feminism, as it was uncommon for young educated women to step beyond the traditional roles of wives and mothers. Her book lets readers witness the frontier life of the early 20th century. A hair-raising section concerns the building of the railroads, which entailed ( 牵涉) drilling through the Rockies, often in blinding snowstorms. The book ends with Rosamond and Dorothy's return to Auburn.

Wickenden is a very good storyteller. The sweep of the land and the stoicism ( 坚忍) of the people move her to some beautiful writing. Here is a picture of Dorothy Woodruff, on her horse, looking down from a hill top:“When the sun slipped behind the mountains, it shed a rosy glow all around them. Then a full moon rose. The snow was marked only by small animals: foxes, coyotes, mice, and varying hares, which turned white in the winter.”
