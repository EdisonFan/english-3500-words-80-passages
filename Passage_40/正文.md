# Passage 40

The memorial to John Todd's environmental legacy lies in his revolutionary eco-machines. When John Todd was a child, he loved to explore the woods around his house, observing how nature solved problems. A dirty stream, for example, often became clear after flowing through plants and along rocks where tiny creatures lived. This is where his journey began as a tale of scientific exploration. When he got older, John started to wonder if this process could be used to clean up the messes people were making.

After studying agriculture, medicine, and fisheries in college, John came to the entrance of the new field of ecological engineering. He went back to observing nature and asking questions. Why can certain plants trap harmful bacteria? Which kinds of fish can eat cancer-causing chemicals? With the right combination of animals and plants, he figured, maybe he could clean up waste the way nature did. He decided to build what he would later call an eco-machine.

The task John set for himself was to remove harmful substances from some sludge. First, he constructed a simple format for organizing a series of clear fiberglass tanks connected to each other. Each tank served as a mini-ecosystem, a vehicle for nature's own purification processes. Then he went around to local ponds and streams and brought back some plants and animals. He placed them in the tanks and waited. Little by little, these different kinds of life got used to one another and formed their own ecosystem. Much like a symphony, these organisms interact in harmony. After a few weeks, John added the sludge.

He was amazed at the results. The plants and animals in the eco-machine took the sludge as food and began to eat it! These living filters work tirelessly to clean the water, requiring minimal human intervention. Within weeks, the sludge had all been digested, and all that was left was pure water. Since then, he has had faith in nature's ability to heal when provided with the right tools.

Over the years John has taken on many big jobs. He developed a greenhouse-like facility that treated sewage from 1,600 homes in the South. He also designed a patent eco-machine to clean canal water in Fuzhou, a city in southeast China.

As we face increasing water challenges in the 21st century, John's eco-machines stand as a testament to the healing power of nature, proving that the answers we seek may already be beneath our feet. 'Ecological design' is the name John gives to what he does. 'Life on Earth is like a box of spare parts for the inventor,' he says. 'You put organisms in new relationships and observe what happens.'
