# Passage 75

Human speech contains more than 2,000 different sounds, from the common "m" and "a" to the rare clicks of some southern African languages. But why are certain sounds more common than others? A ground-breaking, five-year study shows that diet-related changes in human bite led to new speech sounds that are now found in half the world's languages.

Cultural factors have received priority in language studies as their influences might seem straightforward. However, speech sounds can change in a rather passive way. More than 30 years ago, the scholar Charles Hockett noted that speech sounds called labiodentals (唇齿音), such as "f" and "v", were more typical in the languages of societies that ate softer foods. Now a team of researchers led by Damián Blasi at the University of Zurich, Switzerland, has found how and why this trend arose.

They discovered that the arches of upper and lower front teeth of ancient human adults were aligned (对齐), making it hard for the speakers to produce labiodentals, which are formed by touching the lower lip to the upper teeth. Later, our jaws changed to an overbite (上包齿) structure, making it easier to produce such sounds, which is also why abnormal bite patterns like underbite (反颌) or crowded teeth would lead to unclear pronunciation of certain sounds.

The team showed that this change in bite was connected with the development of agriculture in the Neolithic period. Food became easier to chew at this point. We didn't have to eat rough food like bamboo and raw meat anymore. The jawbone didn't have to do as much work and so didn't grow to be so large. The advent (出现) of food processing brought about finer food like bread and rice, prompting the accommodation of our organ structures even further.

Subsequent analysis of a language database also confirmed that there was a global change in the sound of languages after the Neolithic age, with the use of "f" and "v" increasing remarkably during the last few thousand years. These sounds are still not found in the languages of many hunter-gatherer (狩猎采集者) peoples today.

This research overturns the popular view that all human speech sounds were present when human beings evolved around 300,000 years ago. "The set of speech sounds we use has not necessarily remained stable since the appearance of human beings, but rather the huge variety of speech sounds that we find today is the product of a complex interplay of things like biological change and cultural evolution," said Steven Moran, a member of the research team.
