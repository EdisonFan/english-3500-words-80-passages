# Passage 48

The concept of collective intelligence has fascinated researchers and politicians alike. It suggests that large groups of people can make remarkably logical decisions when their judgments are averaged out.

On March 7, 1907, the English statistician Francis Galton published a paper which illustrated what has come to be known as the “wisdom of crowds” effect. The estimation experiment he conducted showed that in some cases, the average of a large number of independent estimates could be quite accurate. This proves that collective intelligence is far more than just a fancy theory.

This effect capitalizes on the fact that when people make errors, those errors aren't always the same. There's a distribution of knowledge and perspectives. Some people will tend to overestimate, and some to underestimate. When enough of these errors are averaged together, they cancel each other out, resulting in a more accurate estimate. In more technical terms, the wisdom of crowds requires that people's estimates be independent, meaning that the sample must include both mighty lions and humble sheep. If for whatever reasons, people's errors become correlated or dependent, the accuracy of the estimate will decline.

But a new study led by Joaquin Navajas offered an interesting twist ( 转折) on this classic phenomenon. The key finding of the study was that when crowds were further divided into smaller groups that were allowed to have a discussion, the averages from these groups became even more accurate. For instance, the average obtained from the estimates of four discussion groups of five members was significantly more accurate than that from 20 independent individuals.

In a follow-up study with 100 university students, the researchers tried to get a better sense of what the group members actually did in their discussion. Did they tend to go with those most confident about their estimates? Did they follow what the majority said? This happened some of the time, but it wasn't the dominant response. Most frequently, the groups reported that they “shared arguments and reasoned together.” Somehow, these arguments and reasoning resulted in a global reduction in error. The research's implications for group discussion and decision-making are enormous.

Modern institutions tend to utilize this phenomenon to predict market trends or vote on consumer preferences. However, the wisdom of crowds isn't permanent. It might fail when faced with complex challenges such as election outcomes. We must remain honest about both the potential and limitations of crowd wisdom after all.
