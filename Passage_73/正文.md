# Passage 73

If you want to tell the history of the whole world, a history that does not privilege one part of humanity, you cannot do it through written documents alone, because most of the world, for most of the time, has never had texts to record their emotions, customs or history. Artifacts, oral traditions, songs, and other non-literate (非文字的) forms of expression provide important insights into social practices, while writing is one of humanity's later achievements. Even many rural areas where people could not read or write recorded their concerns primarily in objects, and only in writing until fairly recently.

We might learn a lot about government policies or wars from historical texts, but these sources often miss the personal stories of ordinary people who lived through those events. Ideally, a history would bring together texts and objects, but in many cases this simply can't be done. The clearest example of this is perhaps the first conflict, at Botany Bay, between Captain Cook's voyage and the Aboriginal Australians. From the English side, we have scientific reports and the military leaders' record of that horrible day. From the Australian side, we have only a wooden shield (盾) dropped by a soldier in the fight after his first experience of gunfire. If we want to reconstruct what was actually going on that day, the shield must be questioned and interpreted as deeply and strictly as the written reports. Furthermore, natural disasters like earthquakes and floods can destroy ancient texts, resulting in gaps in our knowledge of the past.

In addition to the problem of miscomprehension from both sides, there are victories that are accidentally or deliberately twisted, especially when only the victors know how to write. The winning nation would justify their actions, deciding which parts of the story are emphasized and which are forgotten. Those who are defeated often have only objects to tell their stories. The Caribbean Taino (泰诺人), the Aboriginal Australians and other minority groups can speak to us of their past achievements most powerfully through the objects they made. When we consider contact between literate and non-literate societies such as these, all our first-hand accounts are necessarily twisted, only one half of a dialogue. If we are to find the other half of that conversation, we have to read not just the texts, but the objects.
