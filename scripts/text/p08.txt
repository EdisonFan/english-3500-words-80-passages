Not all great writers are great speller. If you want to be publish, it's vital to submit a perfect, professionally presented manuscript. That mean good grammar and no spelling mistakes. No editor is likely to tolerate a writer who does not take the trouble to spell words correctly.

I keep two reference books close by on my desk: a dictionary and a thesaurus. I don't trust my laptop's spellchecker. It takes no account of the context. Of course, these days there are plenty of online dictionaries and thesauruses, but I'm old-fashioned enough to prefer a hard cover and pages I can leaf through with my finger. I use the Concise Oxford Dictionary and the Collins Thesaurus.

Of course, a dictionary is not only for spelling. It should give you a precise definition of each word, thus differentiate it from other words whose meanings are similar, but not identical. It will also usually show how the word is pronounce.

In addition, I have an old two-volume copy of the Shorter Oxford Dictionary, picked up a few years ago in a bookshop sale for just 99 pence. Of course, with its 2,672 pages, it's not exactly short. It contains around 163,000 words, plus word combination and idiomatic phrase. I don't often use this dictionary. However, if I need to check the origin of a word or to look up example of its usage, there's nothing better.

For well over a hundred years the most influential English dictionary was Samuel Johnson's A Dictionary of the English Language published in 1755. " To make dictionaries is dull work, " wrote Johnson, illustrate one definition of " dull " . But a dictionary can be a pleasure to read. A few minutes spent cast your eye over a page or two can be a rewarding experience.
