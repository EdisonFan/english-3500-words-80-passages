Turning soil, pull weed, and harvesting cabbage sound like tough work for middle and high school kid. And at first it is, says Abby Jaramillo, who with another teacher found urban Sprouts, a school garden program at four low-income schools. The program aim to help students develop science skills, environmental awareness, and healthy lifestyle.

Jaramillo's students live in neighborhood where fresh food and green space are not easy to find and fast food restaurant outnumber grocery stores. " The kids literally come to school with bag of snack and large bottle of soft drink, " she says. " They come to us thinking vegetable are awful, dirt is awful, insect are awful. " Though some are initially scared of the insects and turned off by the dirt, most are eager to try something new.

Urban Sprouts' classes, at two middle schools and two high schools, include hands-on experiment such as soil testing, flower-and-seed dissection, tastings of fresh or dried produce, and work in the garden. several times a year, students cook the vegetables they grow, and they occasionally make salad for their entire schools.

Program evaluations show that kids eat more vegetables as a result of the classes. " We have students who say they went home and talked to their parent and now they're eating differently, " Jaramillo says.

She adds that the program's benefits go beyond nutrition. Some students get so interested in gardening that they bring home seed to start their own vegetable gardens. besides, working in the garden seem to have a calming effect on Jaramillo's special education students, many of whom have emotional control issues. " They get outside, " she says, " and they feel successful. "
