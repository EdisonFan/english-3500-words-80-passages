# Passage 55

The Stanford marshmallow ( 棉花糖) test was originally conducted by psychologist Walter Mischel in the late 1960s. This experiment has forever changed our understanding of self-control and delayed gratification. Children aged four to six at a nursery school were placed in a room. A single sugary treat was placed on a table. Each child was told if they waited for 15 minutes before eating the treat, they would be given a second treat. The researcher delivered this message and then exited, leaving the children alone in the room. Some children folded immediately under pressure, while others showed resistance to the temptation. Years later, when researchers inquired about the participants' life outcomes, there was a connection between the ability to postpone gratification and various forms of success.

As adults, we face a version of the marshmallow test every day. We're not tempted ( 诱惑) by sugary treats, but by our computers, phones, and tablets —all the devices that connect us to the global delivery system for various types of information that do to us what marshmallows do to preschoolers. We often find ourselves wrestling with distractions.

We are tempted by sugary treats. One reason is that sweet flavors often indicated ripe fruits like apples or grapes that were safe to eat, while bitter tastes could signal poison. It is not just about safety. Our ancestors lived in a calorie-poor world. Fats and sugars were both crucial for survival, but the latter offered a quick source of energy, which could have distinguished between survival and death. Therefore, our brains developed a response mechanism to sugary treats that reflected their value—a feeling of reward and satisfaction. But as we've reshaped the world around us, sugary desserts and delicious treats became readily available, and we no longer needed to hunt lambs, which dramatically reduced the cost and effort involved in obtaining calories. Yet we still have the same brains we had thousands of years ago, and this mismatch is at the heart of why so many of us struggle to resist tempting foods that we know we shouldn't eat.

A similar process is at work in our response to information. Our formative environment as a species was information-poor, so our brains developed a mechanism that prized new information. But global connectivity has greatly changed our information environment. We are now ceaselessly bombarded ( 轰炸) with new information. With a simple click on the keyboard, we can download an overwhelming amount of data. We surf the internet, subscribe to countless newsletters, and our inboxes fill up with mail, making it increasingly difficult to guard our attention. The digital cloud is not just a place where data is stored; it has made our minds cloudy, too. Therefore, just as we need to be more thoughtful about our caloric consumption, we must learn to swim through the ocean of information without sinking into a soup of meaningless data, resisting the temptation of the mental“ junk food” in order to manage our time most effectively.
